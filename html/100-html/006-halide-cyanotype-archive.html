Halide

Six contact plates of plankton, printed from ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferri-cyanide onto rag paper and developed in running sea water. The sheets are kept flat, unmounted, and in the dark; what follows is the drawer as the bench keeps it — plate numbers, exposure logs, and the bath notes that decided each one.

    Printing blue

    Rerinsing the sheet in the bench tray drives out free Prussian and lifts the line contrast. Applies to the plate on top of the drawer.

    On the process

    Two salts, one sun

    Equal parts ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferri-cyanide are brushed in the shade and dried flat. Ultraviolet reduces Fe(III) to Fe(II) and precipitates insoluble Prussian blue wherever the light reaches; the specimen is laid in direct contact, so nothing is imaged through a lens. What survives the wash is a shadowgram, one-to-one, laterally correct, and irreversible.

    On these sheets

    Every mark measured twice

    Exposure was timed against a standard Wedgwood strip on the same table, and the bath was run from a second watch. Where a plate was re-washed or printed-out afterwards the bench says so in the log rather than quietly correcting the entry. Six sheets are shown here; two hundred and eight remain in the flat file, unnumbered.

    On the wash slider

    The tray is the only developer

    Short washes leave soluble iron in the fibres: the ground greys toward steel and the specimen line loses edge. At ten minutes the blue is at its densest and the whites are clean. Past fifteen the ground begins to go slate and, on the finer strokes, the line goes with it — the same failure that cost us plates III and V.
