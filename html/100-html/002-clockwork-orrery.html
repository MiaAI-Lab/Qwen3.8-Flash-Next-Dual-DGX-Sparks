On the computing of the anomaly

A wheel turned at uniform speed gives a uniform angle, and the heavens are not uniform. The angle the mechanism actually delivers is the mean anomaly M; the angle the planet truly fills, measured at the focus from perihelion, must be extracted from it.

M = E − e sin E Kepler's equation, in circular measure: the mean anomaly M is thrown upon the eccentric orbit by the eccentric anomaly E and the eccentricity e. It cannot be undone algebraically.

The artifice used on this dial is the older one of Newton: begin with the arc itself for a near-circular orbit, or with a semicircle when the orbit is much elongated, then correct the trial by the defect divided by the rate of filling.

En+1 = En − (En − e sin En − M) / (1 − e cos En) Three or four visits are commonly enough; the panel at the right is the live arithmetic for the planet now under the cursor, printed to the thousandth of a degree.

The true anomaly follows without further trial, &tan;(ν/2) = √((1+e)/(1−e)) tan(E/2), and the radius vector as r = a(1 − e cos E). Because the sweep is solved rather than geared, Mercury gains visibly upon the dial as he rounds perihelion in February and drags through aphelion in August, after the manner observed by Kepler and printed in his Astronomia Nova of 1609. The brightened arc drawn behind each planet covers the same interval of time for all of them; its length is the testimony of the second law.

Provenance & remarks

The first clock-work model of the solar system is customarily given to George Graham, the London clockmaker, who set it on his lathe about the year 1704 with John Rowley working the divisions. Rowley built a second copy for Charles Boyle, Earl of Orrery, about 1713, and the nobleman's name has since been borne by every gear-cut attempt at the heavens, to the confusion of spellers and the comfort of dealers.

Graham's model carried but five planets and the Earth's Moon; the dial was turned horizontally and looked down upon like a table. It was Benjamin Martin of Craven Street who, from 1766, pushed the tribe to its grandest exhibition — a three-foot universal orrery, planet tables and all, moved by one arboretum and a fiddle-key governing the pace. His instruments were advertized as teaching “the science of astronomy without the trouble of a telescope”, which is very nearly, and harmlessly, a lie.

This No. 47 belongs to the late reduction: seven worlds, one satellite, and the elements taken from the auxiliary tables then in use. Herschel's Georgium Sidus, discovered in 1781, is admitted upon its proper wheel; Neptune, found by Le Verrier and Adams in 1846, is deliberately omitted — the frame will not carry the eighth arc, and the maker preferred an honest omission to a false arc. The long ellipticities are preserved, not corrected: nothing on this dial is geared to a circle.

Use. Turn the handle slowly, or scrub the dial with the finger, and observe that the escapement is an indication and not a tyrant — hold it and the planets abide exactly where the ephemeris has left them, which is the whole merit of a solved anomaly over a counted one. The plate raised over any planet gives the period, the eccentricity, and the anomaly it is at this instant filling.
