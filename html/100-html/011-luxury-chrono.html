Maison Nocturne — Genève

N-14

One watch. Made impeccably.

We make a single reference. Thirty-nine millimetres of brushed steel, a champagne guilloché dial turned on a rose engine that has been in Genève since 1874, and hands blued to a deep afternoon-sky blue by hand, over a bed of charcoal.

Everything you see on this page is drawn, not photographed. Scroll slowly — the watch will take itself apart.

Live indications · 28 800 A/h simulated at 8 beats per second

01 — Light

A dial that holds a low lamp.

The guilloché is cut by hand, one pass at a time: eight passes of a grain d’orge tool, each removing a tenth of a millimetre of gold-plated brass. Under a low lamp the pattern swallows the light at the edges and returns it at the centre — like a field after rain.

Over it sits a box sapphire, 2.1 millimetres, with two layers of anti-reflective coating on both faces. Move your cursor across it.

02 — Readability

Blued steel, applied gold.

The hands are polished steel heated to 297 °C on a brass bed, then rinsed in alcohol — the oxide layer that reads as blue is thirty wavelengths thin. The indices are solid gold, applied by hand with two pins each, their chamfers burnished with a steel agate until they throw a single hard line of light.

The hour hand you are reading is drawn at this moment, from your own clock.

03 — Under the dial

Calibre N-14.

Two hundred and twenty-eight parts. A free-sprung balance, 31 ruby jewels, a bidirectional rotor on ceramic ball bearings, and seventy-two hours of reserve from a single barrel — enough to carry Friday night to Monday morning without a winder.

04 — Notes

On the escapement, and other patient mechanisms.

The anchor’s two meetings

Sixty-four times a second, somewhere under the dial, a steel pallet fork changes its mind. The escape wheel advances one tooth, the fork rocks, and the balance receives the smallest possible push — a whisper of torque, timed to land a quarter-turn after the balance crosses the centre.

That is all a lever escapement is: a quarrel, finely negotiated, between a wheel that wants to unwind and a balance that wants to keep time. Everything else on the movement exists to keep the argument honest — the jewels to still friction, the regulator to shorten the hairspring’s working length by a few tenths of a millimetre, the shock springs to catch the watch when the world drops it.

Note I · Escapement — calibre N-14

The balance and the breath

A free-sprung balance carries no index pins. Its rate is set by two gold poising screws and by nothing else, which is slower to adjust and far less likely to lie. The hairspring rises into a Breguet overcoil so that it breathes concentrically — centre in, centre out — rather than strangling itself at the stud.

Adjusted to five positions and two temperatures, the N-14 is delivered with a certificate that reads like a medical chart: dial up, dial down, crown left, crown right, crown down. We keep the trace. A watch is a small patient, and it is our job to know its moods.
