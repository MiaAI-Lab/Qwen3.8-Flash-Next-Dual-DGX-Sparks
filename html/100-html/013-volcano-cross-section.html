Chapter I

TheEdifice

Every band you can see is one year, or one century, or one very bad week.

Climb the section from the basement and the cone resolves into a couple of hundred alternating leaves: dark, coherent lava flows that poured down the flank and froze in a day, and pale, loose beds of ash and pumice that fell around the vent and washed down as sediment the first time it rained. Neither layer is horizontal. Both dip away from the vent at 12–28°, the angle at which a pile of angular fragments and clinker stops sliding.

Because the conduit rarely moves more than a kilometre in 400 000 years, the stack grows vertically as much as laterally, and the cone steepens until gravity intervenes. Then a flank fails, and the volcano loses a sector the size of a small city.

Chapter I · continued

Interbedding

Hover any stratum in the section. The label gives the rock as a mapping geologist would write it: the lithology, the transport process, and a thickness. The difference between a flow and a fall is the whole discipline — one was poured, one was dropped out of the sky.

Where a bed is cut by a hairline of dark rock, that is a dike: a sheet of magma injected along the bedding plane or across it, typically 1–4 m wide, fed from the same chamber and usually the last thing that moved before an eruption. The flat, tabular bodies stacked under the summit are sills — magma that chose the path of least resistance sideways instead of upward.

A conduit is a rumour until the dike swarm arrives.

Chapter II

TheConduit

Between 6 and 11 km down sits the thing the mountain is built on top of.

The chamber is not a cavern of liquid rock. It is a framework of plagioclase, pyroxene and magnetite crystals — 40–60 % by volume — with interstitial melt, a crystal mush that behaves as a solid until the melt fraction crosses roughly 0.5 and then, abruptly, as a liquid you can erupt. The stipple in the section is that mush: grains suspended in liquid too viscous to convect.

Above it the conduit narrows from a few hundred metres to a throat of 15–40 m. Water exsolves at about 120 MPa, bubbles nucleate, the melt froths, density collapses, and the column accelerates. Between eruptions the throat freezes: crystal-laden magma stalls, degasses and welds into a plug, and the cap rock — a sill complex of baked hornfels and impermeable dacite — seals the system shut. Pressure rebuilds over years to decades.

Chapter II · continued

Plug &Cap rock

The plug is the reason Vulcanian eruptions are sudden. A permeable conduit vents passively and quietly; a blocked one does not. Gas percolates out of the mush and accumulates beneath the seal until the rock fails in a brittle fracture, and the whole throat decompresses at once — a blast that shatters the plug into lapilli and rings the cone with fresh fallout.

Seismic networks see this coming as days of long-period events and tremor, the plumbing ticking as it fills. Tilt meters read a few microradians of inflation each season, the chamber taking on magma the volume of a thousand Olympic pools and losing most of it again to crystallisation.

Chapter III

TheEruption

Fragmentation is a phase change in behaviour, not in chemistry.

Rise the index in the instrument panel and the column answers. What changes is not the colour of the magma but the fraction of gas in it and the speed at which the throat can deliver: at VEI 2 the plume is a grey, density-driven chimney that collapses every few hundred metres; at VEI 6 it is an incandescent curtain that punches the tropopause and spreads an umbrella cloud against the stratospheric wind.

Above the vent the column is buoyant because it is hot and because it is full of entrained air — a turbulent jet that entrains several tonnes of atmosphere for every tonne it carries. Where the bulk density matches the surrounding air it stops rising and flows sideways. That level, not the height of the fireworks, is what aviation cares about.

The shear you can see bending the top of the column is real: 40–90 kt of upper-level wind tilts the jet 20–45° downwind and elongates the dispersal ellipse by a factor of three.

Chapter III · continued

Readingthe index

The Volcanic Explosivity Index is logarithmic in bulk ejecta volume, one order of magnitude per step, and it saturates: nobody has measured an eruption big enough to deserve a 9 in the last 26 000 years. It is a crude scale and volcanologists use it anyway, because it is the only one that lets a layperson compare Tambora with Etna.

Two caveats the panel cannot show. First, volume without duration is meaningless — 1 km³ in nine hours is a different disaster from 1 km³ in thirteen months. Second, the index says nothing about hazard: a VEI 3 cone sitting under 400 000 people outranks a VEI 6 in a national park.

Chapter IV

Aftermath

The rock is dead for a decade and useful for a million years.

Tephra is not soil. It is ground-up rock with no organic matter, no water-holding capacity and no seed bank, and it drains so fast that a bare ash field can be drier at 10 cm depth than the air above it. Succession therefore starts from zero, in the geological sense of the word, and it starts with things that can fix their own nitrogen.

On real deposits — Coseguintin after 1943, Mount St. Helens after 1980, Katmai after 1912 — the sequence repeats with uncomfortable regularity: crust and lichen, then a lupine or a willowherb, then alder along the wet lahars, then conifers, then a soil horizon you can cut with a spade. Two centuries buys you a forest on andesite; two thousand buys you a slope that finally holds water.

Ash is the fastest thing to become a meadow.

Dates are median field observations from ice-free tephra blankets 0.4–4 m thick, 45–62° paler latitude, mean annual precipitation 1 400 mm. Where the deposit is thinner than 30 cm, succession runs roughly twice as fast and skips the pioneer entirely.

0YR

Bare tephra

Distal ash 0.4–4 m, still warm at depth. pH 8.4, < 0.2 % organics, zero available nitrogen. No living propagules survive the blanket.

2YR

Crust & crustaceans

Rain builds a 5 mm sedimentary crust that stops deflation. Brine shrimp and fairy shrimp hatch in ephemeral pools; their eggs arrive on wind.

6YR

Moss & lichen

Racomitrium and Cladonia hold the surface. Biological soil crust reaches 12 % cover and fixes the first 0.4 g N m⁻² yr⁻¹.

12YR

Lupine banks

Lupinus lepidus colonises by itself, nodulated by Bradyrhizobium. Single plants die in place; dense patches raise nitrogen 40-fold and are the true turning point.

45YR

Alder on lahars

Alnus incana follows the water down lahar corridors, fixes 8–15 g N m⁻² yr⁻¹, and drops a litter layer that becomes the first honest A-horizon.

200YR

Conifer canopy

Trees to 22 m, canopy closed, 40 cm of soil with a recognisable profile. The vent is invisible. The chamber beneath it has begun to cool.
